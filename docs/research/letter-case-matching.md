# Letter case matching: research for a capital-to-lowercase Game, ages 3 to 5

Researched 2026-09-29 for a new Game or Topic on the shelf: the child matches lowercase letters to the capitals she already names (A with a). Drag, feedback, rewards, session length and learning from screens are covered in [toddler-sorting-game.md](./toddler-sorting-game.md) and built into `@shared/sort`. This doc cites them only where letters change the answer.

## Summary

Children learn capitals first and lowercase letters later, mostly by carrying over what they know about the capital. A preschooler who names a capital is more than 16 times as likely to name its lowercase letter, and a lowercase letter shaped like its capital (c, o, s, x) is named about three times as often as one that isn't (a, g, r). So the Game should start from the capitals and the look-alike pairs. The main task is sorting lowercase Things into Bins that show capitals. That fits the shared sorting screen and the user's daughter's love of dragging. For a look-alike pair the match is almost identical-picture matching. For a pair that looks different, the Bin first shows the lowercase letter faintly beside the capital, and the hint fades as she gets it right. Put the 26 pairs in four Ranges: Look-alikes (c o s v w x z), Nearly alike (i j k p u y), Different (a e f h m n r t) and Tricky (b d g l q). Preschool test data rank the Ranges from easiest to hardest in that order. Mirror confusions of b, d, p and q are normal up to 6 or 7. Keep confusable letters apart in the same Round until the Tricky Range. Use 2 Bins, then 3. Say only the letter name, the same word for both cases, once per Bin per Round. Never say "lowercase", "capital" or "little". Draw every letter in Andika (SIL, Open Font License), which has single-story a and g by default and an I, l and 1 that don't look alike. Set both cases on one shared baseline, so p and q hang below it and b and d rise above. For the clips, one US speaker saying all 26 letter names is on Freesound under CC BY 4.0. Wikimedia Commons covers all 26 too, but with about ten different speakers.

## 1. When children learn lowercase letters, and which ones come first

- **Capitals come first.** Studies in several countries find capitals named before lowercase letters ([Vinter et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/10409289.2023.2252706), citing Worden & Boettcher 1990, Evans et al. 2006 and Treiman & Kessler 2004). An item-response analysis of 1,113 US preschoolers, mean age about 4 years 2 months, put lowercase letters at 0.76 logits of difficulty against 0.28 for capitals ([Bowles et al. 2014](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED613911.pdf), Table 2 and p. 151). The Texas pre-K guide says the same thing to teachers: capitals are "more distinguishable" ([TEA 2022](https://tea.texas.gov/sites/default/files/2022-texas-pkg-comprehensive-guide.pdf), p. 36).
- **How many, and when.** Worden & Boettcher found that 4-year-olds from middle-income families named 54% of letters (via [Phillips et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4322910/)). In France, where children know fewer letters at these ages, 4 to 5-year-olds named about 20% of printed lowercase letters, and 5 to 6-year-olds about 39% (Vinter et al., Table 1). A benchmark study of 371 US preschoolers found that knowing 18 capitals and 15 lowercase letters at the end of preschool best predicted first-grade reading ([Piasta, Petscher & Justice 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027757)). Head Start now uses those numbers (section 5). A 3-year-old who already names her capitals is ahead of these samples.
- **Lowercase letters ride on capitals.** Turnbull, Bowles, Skibbe, Justice & Wiggins tested 461 low-income 3 to 5-year-olds. Knowing the capital was the strongest predictor: a child was "more than 16 times more likely to know a lowercase letter if they knew the corresponding uppercase letter". Similarity between the cases and frequency in print also predicted, and similarity mattered more when the capital was known ([Turnbull et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1044/1092-4388(2010/09-0093)); I read only the abstract).
- **Shape similarity is the main letter-level effect.** In Vinter et al., lowercase letters that look like their capitals were named correctly 46.6% of the time and the others 14.6% (p. 14). Adults sorted each pair as similar or not. The similar set was c i j k o p s u v w x y z, with f and j borderline, 40 to 60%. Treiman & Kessler's 2003 set is narrower: c k o p s v w x z. Among 5,020 US kindergarteners, similarity to the capital raised the odds of naming a lowercase letter, and resembling other letters in shape or name lowered them ([Huang & Invernizzi 2014](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0142716412000604), abstract only). Every study that tested similarity found the effect (Vinter et al., p. 3). This is strong, consistent evidence, but it is correlational.
- **The child's own name.** Children were 1.5 times as likely to know a letter in their first name and more than 11 times as likely to know its first letter (Justice et al. 2006, via Phillips et al.). For capitals, name letters were the main driver. For lowercase letters, similarity was (Vinter et al.). The user's daughter's name gives K, A and I. k is a look-alike, i is nearly alike, and a is different.
- **Per-letter difficulty.** Bowles et al.'s lowercase difficulties, easiest first: o x c s k w z i e y m p r f v u j a n h t g b l d q. The four hardest are q, d, l and b, all confusable with another letter.

**Design implication:** Order by shape stability, as Vinter et al. suggest (p. 18). Four Ranges cover all 26 pairs, and their mean lowercase difficulty in Bowles et al.'s data rises in order:

| Range | Pairs | Why together | Mean difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Look-alikes | C O S V W X Z | the lowercase letter is a smaller copy | 0.25 |
| Nearly alike | I J K P U Y | one change: a dot, a tail, a drop below the line | 0.61 |
| Different | A E F H M N R T | the lowercase letter must be learned | 0.84 |
| Tricky | B D G L Q | mirror or look-alike partners (b d q, g q, l I 1) | 1.53 |

## 2. Confusable letters, mirror errors and distractors

- **Mirror errors are normal.** Letter reversals are common in beginning readers, and the evidence that they mark dyslexia is mixed. Children aged 3 to 7 will often write their name backwards if they start near the right-hand margin ([Blackburne et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0098386), introduction). The usual account is that seeing a mirror image as the same thing is useful for objects, and reading has to switch it off for b, d, p and q. In children aged 5 to 12, early brain responses did not yet tell normal letters from reversed ones, while adults' did. About 95% of 356 French 5 to 6-year-olds reversed at least one letter or digit when writing from memory. Copying produced far fewer reversals ([Fischer & Luxembourger 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcomm.2021.719652), reviewing Fischer & Tazouti 2012). At 3 to 5, confusing b with d is expected, not a problem to fix.
- **Which differences children see first.** Gibson et al. had 4 to 8-year-olds match letter-like shapes against changed versions. A break or a closed gap was the easiest difference to spot. Rotation and reversal were middling at 4 and improved with age ([Gibson et al. 1962](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0043190); I read only secondary summaries). So c against o (a gap) is easy, and b against d (a mirror image) is hard.
- **Errors follow shape, and name.** Among 687 US and Brazilian preschoolers naming capitals, shape similarity was "the major determinant of confusion errors", especially for letters similar in both shape and name ([Treiman, Kessler & Pollo 2006](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0142716406060255), abstract). Bowles et al. found b, d, p, q and l to be the weakest items for telling who knows letters and who doesn't.
- **Distractors early and late.** Vinter et al. advise against "mixing letters whose shapes may be confused, at least in the initial steps of learning" (p. 18). I found no study of distractor similarity in a case-matching task itself. The closest is errorless training. 108 preschoolers learned letter discriminations with fewer errors, and did better on an immediate and a delayed test, when training highlighted the distinctive feature and then faded the highlight. Plain right/wrong feedback did worse, and so did a cue that ignored the distinctive feature ([Egeland 1975](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0076774), abstract).

**Design implication:** Never put two Bins in one Round whose lowercase letters are a confusable pair: b d p q, g q, n u, m w, h n, n r, i j l, f t, a o, a d, c e, u v y, v w. The capitals M W, E F, P R, O Q, C G and U V follow the same rule. My Letter already keeps B from D and M from W. The Tricky Range brings them together on purpose, but only after each letter has been matched in easier Rounds. When a confusable pair first meets, highlight the part that differs (the bump of b and of d) and fade it, as Egeland did.

## 3. The task: sorting into capital Bins, finding, pairs

- **Sorting to the capital uses what she knows.** The capital is the known form (Turnbull et al.), so the Bin shows the capital and the Things are lowercase letters. Identical-picture matching comes before arbitrary matching in toddlers (sorting doc, Sandhofer & Smith 1999). A look-alike pair is nearly identical-picture matching. A different pair (A and a) is an arbitrary link at first. Texas lists "match upper and lowercase letters, while recalling the name of the letters" as a behavior of 4-year-olds (TEA 2022, p. 36).
- **Scaffold and fade.** For a Different or Tricky pair, show the lowercase letter faintly beside the capital on the Bin, "Aa", so the first matches are identity matches, then fade it. This follows errorless training by fading a prompt (Egeland 1975), not a test of this exact design. It suits a child who outgrows scaffolds fast, since the fade is driven by her own right drops.
- **Find the letter.** California's foundation for about 60 months is to "match more than half of uppercase letter names and more than half of lowercase letter names to their printed form" ([CDE 2008, Foundation 3.2](https://www.cde.ca.gov/SP/CD/re/documents/psfoundvol1appendix.doc)). That is Find It's task: hear "a", tap the a. It needs the name clips (section 6).
- **Fewer choices, better memory.** 30-month-olds picked a named new object equally well among 2, 3 or 4 competitors, but only those with few competitors remembered the names later ([Horst, Scott & Pollard 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-7687.2009.00926.x); coin doc section 4). Use 2 Bins, then 3.
- **Spacing.** Thirty-six 3-year-olds (mean 42.8 months) learned more when examples were spaced than when they were massed, both for identical objects and for categories ([Vlach, Sandhofer & Kornell 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2008.07.013)). The Scope rule that every item comes up once before any comes again already spaces the letters.
- **Pairs (memory) and "mama and baby letters".** I found no study of either for letter learning at 3 to 5. A face-down pairs game mostly tests spatial memory, not the case link. The mama-and-baby story fits the Look-alikes, where the lowercase letter really is a smaller copy. It stops fitting at A and a, and it teaches that size is what makes a letter lowercase. It could be a Skin, never the words the game says.
- **Screens.** A meta-analysis of 36 app studies found +0.31 SD overall, with larger effects for preschoolers and for constrained skills such as letter knowledge ([Kim et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/23328584211004183)). In a randomized trial with 48 children aged 2 to 5, nine weeks of letter matching, tracing and drawing apps raised letter-name and letter-sound knowledge over a waitlist ([Neumann 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecresq.2017.10.006)). That is small, but relevant.

**Design implication:** The main task is Sort: lowercase letters into capital Bins. The second is a Find It Way, "Find the a" among 2 or 3 lowercase letters. Leave out a pairs game for now. The reverse, capitals into lowercase Bins, is a later variation for a child bored by the same task.

## 4. Typography

- **Use a real literacy font.** The site font is `ui-rounded, 'SF Pro Rounded', 'Nunito', 'Varela Round', system-ui` (`src/shared/base.css`). It resolves to different fonts on different devices, so the a a child learns could change from phone to laptop. A letter game has to ship its own letter shapes.
- **Andika.** SIL designed Andika "especially for use as a literacy font", for beginning readers. The design page lists single-story a and g, "capital i, lower case l, and numeral 1 that don't look alike", and an r that doesn't join n into m ([SIL, Andika design](https://software.sil.org/andika/design/)). I rendered version 6.101 and confirmed single-story a and g, a serifed I, a plain l and a flagged 1. Its x-height is 0.70 of the cap height, so c o s x stay visibly smaller than their capitals at one font size. Stylistic sets switch in double-story a and g (ss01, or ss13 and ss14 separately). cv39 gives l a curved tail ([SIL, Andika features](https://software.sil.org/andika/features/)).
- **Andika's licence.** SIL Open Font License 1.1, with "Andika" and "SIL" as Reserved Font Names. Andika is on Google Fonts as Regular, Italic, Bold and Bold Italic ([google/fonts METADATA](https://github.com/google/fonts/blob/main/ofl/andika/METADATA.pb)). Self-hosting the files unchanged is fine. Subsetting counts as modification and then the name may not be used (OFL-FAQ 2.6). Graphics made with an OFL font are not under the OFL (OFL-FAQ 1.13). But FAQ 1.14 discourages pulling outlines out of a font, so pre-rendering the 52 letters to SVG is a grey area. Serving the unchanged font file is the clean choice.
- **Alternatives.** ABeeZee (Anja Meiners, OFL, Google Fonts) is "a children's learning font", with single-story a and g and a tailed l, but only one weight. Sassoon Primary and Sassoon Infant are sold commercially ([fonts.com](https://www.fonts.com/font/sassoon-williams/sassoon-primary)). I rendered Playpen Sans (TypeTogether, OFL) too: its default I and l are identical strokes, so it is unsuitable.
- **Single- or double-story a.** In 48 six-year-olds reading text set with and without "infant characters" (single-story a and g), the forms made no statistically significant difference ([Walker & Reynolds 2003](https://doi.org/10.1075/idj.11.2.04wal)). Walker adds that most children knew both forms: "a is what we write and a is what we read" ([Walker, Books for Keeps](https://booksforkeeps.co.uk/article/typography-in-childrens-books/)). The sample is small and the children were older than 3 to 5.
- **Baseline and size.** I found no study of baselines in letter matching. The case for one is geometric. With each letter centred in its tile, p is d turned upside down and b is q turned. On a shared baseline, p and q hang below and b and d rise above, and that position is a second cue besides the mirror.

**Design implication:** Draw letters in Andika Bold, capitals and lowercase at one font size on one baseline, with a faint line under them. Never centre each glyph's own box in its tile. Default to single-story a and g, the forms children write. Add double-story a and g later as extra Things that also go in the A and G Bins. Turn on cv39 (tailed l) so l never looks like I or 1.

## 5. What to call them, and what standards expect

- **Standards.** Common Core RF.K.1d: "Recognize and name all upper- and lowercase letters of the alphabet" by the end of kindergarten, at about 6 ([CCSS RF.K](https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/RF/K/)). Head Start, by 60 months: "Names 18 upper- and 15 lower-case letters" (Goal P-LIT 3; [Head Start ELOF](https://eclkc.ohs.acf.hhs.gov/school-readiness/article/literacy-preschool)). The 36 to 48-month indicator is only awareness: "recognizing letters from one's name, or naming some letters". California, at about 60 months: more than half of each case (Foundation 3.2). Texas PK4, by the end of pre-K: "recognizes and names at least 20 letters (upper- or lower-case letters)" (TEA 2022, p. 36). So lowercase matching at 3 is ahead of every standard. That is fine for a child who already names her capitals, and it means there is no rush.
- **Words.** The standards say "upper- and lowercase". Texas also says "capital letters". I found no research on which term helps a preschooler. Classroom advice against "big" and "little" is common but comes from teacher blogs, not studies.

**Design implication:** The child hears only letter names. For the grown-up, label Ranges and Ways with shapes, "ABC → abc", and use "capital" and "lowercase" in any grown-up text.

## 6. Audio: what to say, and free clips

- **Say the name, once.** A capital and its lowercase letter share one name, and that shared name is what links them. Vinter et al. suggest showing both cases with the name at the same time (pp. 17–18), a prediction they had not tested. The user's daughter tires of hearing the same sound over and over, so follow the sorting rule already in place: a right drop says the Bin's name, "a", the first time in a Round, and after that only the landing Sound plays. In Find, say only the target, "a". Never say "little a" or "lowercase a".
- **Freesound: one speaker, all 26.** tim.kahn's pack [The Alphabet](https://freesound.org/people/tim.kahn/packs/4371/) is a US English female voice saying a to z, 26 files (sounds 67712 to 67737, 2009). I checked a, i and z: each is CC BY 4.0. The files run about 2 s with silence and need the usual trim and level.
- **Wikimedia Commons: all 26, about ten speakers.** [Category:U.S. English pronunciation of letters](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:U.S._English_pronunciation_of_letters) has 34 files (Commons API, 2026-09-29). Twocs uploaded 17 together on 2009-04-16: b c d f g h l m o q s t u v w x y (En-us-b.ogg and so on, CC BY-SA 3.0). The rest come from other speakers: a (Xnux, public domain), e and r (Logictheo, CC BY-SA 3.0), i (Geoff Sebastian, public domain), j, n, p and z (Dvortygirl, CC BY-SA 3.0), k (Neskaya, CC BY-SA 3.0). There are extras from Muke (A, I; public domain), Sylvanmoon (CC BY-SA 4.0) and Paul2520 (double-u, CC BY 4.0).
- **Lingua Libre (Commons).** Grendelkhan (CC0) has single-letter files only for a, l and o, and "a" may be the article. Homophones such as bee, see, gee, eye, jay, em, oh, pee, are, tea, you, ex and why bring Grendelkhan to 15 letters. d e f h k n q s v w z are missing. Vealhurl (CC BY-SA 4.0) spelled out 13 names (ay, cee, dee, ef, aitch...). No Lingua Libre speaker has all 26.
- **Others.** OpenGameArt's [A-Z Male Voice](https://opengameart.org/content/a-z-male-voice) (Brannon Wyndesor, CC BY-SA 3.0) has 26 letters. I did not check its accent. Feed The Monster, the source of My Letter's clips, has letter sounds but no letter names.

**Design implication:** Use the tim.kahn pack, trimmed and levelled by a Game task like My Letter's and credited under CC BY 4.0. The same 26 clips would also give My Letter and Find It letter names on devices without speech.

## Recommendations for the spec

1. The Game teaches the link from each known capital to its lowercase letter. It does not teach letter sounds or writing.
2. Main task: Sort. Lowercase Things go into 2 Bins, then 3, each Bin showing a capital. All the `@shared/sort` rules carry over: tap a Bin to place, a Miss glides back and lights the right Bin, the Thing goes in by itself after two Misses, no buzzer.
3. Ranges on Practice, one tap each: Look-alikes (C O S V W X Z), Nearly alike (I J K P U Y), Different (A E F H M N R T), Tricky (B D G L Q), and All. If the Game knows the child's name, add a Range for its letters.
4. A Round never puts confusable letters in Bins together (section 2), except in Tricky, and only once each letter has been matched elsewhere.
5. For Different and Tricky pairs, the Bin shows the faint lowercase letter beside the capital at first. It fades after the child has placed that letter with no Miss in two Rounds.
6. A right drop plays the landing Sound and, the first time in a Round, the letter-name clip. Nothing says "capital", "lowercase", "big" or "little".
7. Second task: a Find It Way, "Find the a", with 2 or 3 lowercase letters and only the target named.
8. Letters are Andika Bold (OFL), self-hosted unmodified with its OFL.txt. Single-story a and g, tailed l (cv39), both cases at one size on one visible baseline.
9. Later variations: double-story a and g as extra Things, and capitals sorted into lowercase Bins.
10. Name clips: tim.kahn's The Alphabet (Freesound, CC BY 4.0), trimmed and credited.

## Open questions and weak evidence

- **Correlational, and mostly older children.** Similarity, capital transfer and name effects come from naming tests of 4 to 6-year-olds. No study has taught lowercase letters in similarity order and compared it with another order (Vinter et al. call for one). The Ranges are a reasoned proposal.
- **The Range boundaries.** f and j were borderline in Vinter's adult ratings. u, y, i and j are similar in Vinter's set but not in Treiman & Kessler's. v is a Look-alike by shape but middling in Bowles' data.
- **No case-matching study.** I found none on match-to-capital tasks, distractor similarity in them, pairs games or "mama and baby" framing at this age. The fading scaffold adapts Egeland 1975 and is untested in this form.
- **Baseline** is a geometric argument with no study behind it.
- **Single- vs double-story a** evidence is one small UK reading study with 6-year-olds.
- **Abstract or secondary only:** Turnbull et al. 2010, Huang & Invernizzi 2014, Treiman et al. 2006, Egeland 1975, Gibson et al. 1962, Worden & Boettcher 1990 and Justice et al. 2006. Full text read: Vinter et al. 2023, Bowles et al. 2014, Phillips et al. 2012, Vlach et al. 2008 and Blackburne et al. 2014.
- **Clips unheard.** I checked licences and counts through the APIs and pages but did not listen. Whether Grendelkhan's "a" is the letter, and how tim.kahn's clips sound, needs a listen.
