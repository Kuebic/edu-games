# Toddler category sorting: research for a "Kinds" Topic in Tidy Up, ages 2 to 4

Researched 2026-09-29 for a new Topic in Tidy Up: the child sorts pictures of Things into Bins by kind (animals, food, plants, vehicles), later by finer kinds (birds vs mammals, fruit vs vegetables). Touch input, feedback style, rewards, session length and screen transfer are covered in [toddler-sorting-game.md](toddler-sorting-game.md) and are not repeated here.

## Summary

Big, far-apart kinds come first, not "dogs vs cats". Toddlers separate animals from vehicles, furniture and plants in their second year. At that age they barely separate dogs from rabbits or cars from motorbikes. But a picture sort into big kinds is still shaky at 3. In Rosch's triads, 3-year-olds got 55% right on animal-vs-vehicle pairs, and 4-year-olds got 96%. Three-year-olds were at chance on fruit vs vegetables. So Level 1 is animals vs vehicles, the widest contrast. Plants vs animals comes next. Fruit vs vegetables is a 4-to-5-year-old contrast at best. A spoken category word helps. Novel nouns push toddlers toward sorting by kind rather than by theme, so every Bin gets a word, even one the child doesn't know yet, such as "vehicles". Superordinate words behave like plural or mass words, so the Word is plural: "Animals!", "Toys!". A Bin should show two or three small varied pictures, not a single exemplar and not a place. A barn or a kitchen invites "goes with" sorting. Leave out atypical members (penguin, whale, bat, teddy bear, mushroom, tomato) until a later, clearly labeled round.

## 1. Development: when each level appears

- **Global kinds come first in infancy, not basic-level ones.** In an object-examination task, 9- and 11-month-olds treated animals and vehicles as different kinds. Within animals they did not separate dogs from fish or rabbits. They did separate birds from airplanes, although the models shared outstretched wings ([Mandler & McDonough 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0885-2014(93)80003-C), abstract only). A later set of 5 experiments found 7 to 11-month-olds separated animals, vehicles and furniture. Plants and kitchen utensils were separated at 11 months, and kitchen utensils were not separated at 9. Dogs vs birds was separated at 9 and 11 months, dogs vs cats only at 11. Tables, chairs and beds were never separated ([Mandler & McDonough 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9823512/), abstract).
- **Sequential touching shows the same order at 1½ to 2 years.** With small models, 1½ and 2-year-olds grouped animals, vehicles, plants, furniture and eating utensils. Even at 2 they did not group dogs apart from rabbits, cars from motorcycles, trees from cacti, tables from chairs, or spoons from forks. The only finer split was life-form: dogs vs birds or fish, and cars vs airplanes ([Mandler 2000 review](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~jmandler/abstract/PercConcCats00.pdf), summarising [Mandler & Bauer 1988](https://doi.org/10.1016/0885-2014(88)90011-1) and [Mandler, Bauer & McDonough 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(91)90011-C); the originals are paywalled). Two basic kinds are easy to tell apart when they come from different domains, such as dogs and cars, and hard when they share one, such as dogs and horses (Mandler & Bauer 1988, abstract summary).
- **Picture sorts by superordinate kind are still fragile at 3.** In Rosch's oddity triads with photos, children picked "the two that are the same kind of thing". 3-year-olds (mean 3;5) were 99% right when a basic-level pair was available (two cats plus a car). They were 55% right when only a superordinate pair was (a cat, a fish and a car), against 33% chance. 4-year-olds (mean 4;7) scored 96%. There were 10 children per condition ([Rosch et al. 1976, Exp. 8](https://www.cns.nyu.edu/~msl/courses/2223/Readings/Rosch-CogPsych1976.pdf)). Sorting ran ahead of naming: 3-year-olds named even the basic pairs correctly only 65% of the time.
- **Food.** In a 2-choice picture task, 3-year-olds (n = 16) were 60% right on both taxonomic triads (fruit, meat, dairy) and script triads (breakfast, birthday foods), against 50% chance. They were above chance only on dairy, meats and birthday foods. They were not above chance on fruit, vegetables, breads or drinks. 4-year-olds were 70% right overall ([Nguyen & Murphy 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2442888/)).
- **Plants are a visual kind early but a "living thing" late.** Infants separate plant models from animals by 11 months (Mandler & McDonough 1998). But the idea that plants and animals are both alive comes years later. In a sort by "alive", 4-year-olds (English n = 56, Indonesian n = 46) mostly put humans and animals together and left plants out. Under "die" and "grow", a living-thing sort that included plants was the main pattern by 6. English-speaking 9-year-olds still often left plants out of "alive" ([Anggoro, Waxman & Medin 2008](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/medin/documents/AnggoroWaxmanMedinNaming2008.pdf)). Preschoolers treat goal-directed movement as a sign of life ([Opfer & Siegler 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2004.01.002), abstract only).
- **"Animal" is narrow for young children.** English 6-year-olds read "animal" as excluding people 86% of the time (Anggoro et al. 2008, Exp. 1).

**Design implication:** A 2-year-old can sort animals from vehicles. A 3-year-old is right at the edge on pictures, and a 4-year-old is solid. "Plants" works as a looks-alike kind (trees, flowers), not as "living things". Fruit vs vegetables is not a 3-year-old contrast.

## 2. Which contrasts first

- **The widest contrast is easiest.** Animals vs vehicles is the best-tested and earliest pair, from about 9 months for models (Mandler & McDonough 1993) to 96% of 4-year-olds on photos (Rosch 1976). Animals vs furniture and animals vs plants come next in infancy data (Mandler & McDonough 1998). Kitchen utensils came later than furniture.
- **Pairs inside one domain are hard,** even when the pair looks like a basic contrast: dogs vs cats, cars vs motorbikes, spoons vs forks (Mandler 2000). Mammals vs birds is the one within-animal split toddlers make early. Dogs vs birds is separated at 9 months (Mandler & McDonough 1998), and 2-year-olds separate dogs from birds or fish (Mandler 2000). That makes land animals vs birds, or animals vs fish, the natural second-tier animal round, well before any "mammal" word.
- **Fruit vs vegetables is fuzzy.** 3-year-olds were at chance (Nguyen & Murphy 2003). The adult boundary is cultural: tomato, cucumber, pepper, avocado and corn are fruits or grains botanically but vegetables in the kitchen. Waxman & Namy's own stimuli paired a carrot with a tomato as the "same kind" ([1997](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.33.3.555)).
- **Typical members first.** Children learn and extend categories from typical members before atypical ones ([Rosch et al. 1976](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(76)90013-X); [Mervis & Pani 1980](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(80)90018-3), not read in full). Atypical members that break the kind's usual look are the risky ones: penguin (a bird that swims), bat (a mammal that flies), whale and dolphin (mammals that look like fish), butterfly and bee (young children may not call insects animals), mushroom (neither plant nor food in a toddler's eyes, and the emoji is a toadstool), and people.
- **"Home things" is not a kind.** No lexical category or infant study covers it. Mandler's domains are furniture and kitchen utensils. "Home things" is a place-based, script-like grouping, and a pet dog belongs to it thematically. "Toys" and "clothes" are real, named superordinate kinds (see section 3). "Food" is also early, but see section 4 for its thematic traps.

**Design implication:** Ladder by distance between kinds. Animals vs vehicles, then animals vs plants, then 3 bins, then within-domain life-forms (land animals vs birds, then vs fish), and fruit vs vegetables last with only textbook members.

## 3. Labels and Bin pictures for pre-readers

- **Words invite kinds.** 12 to 13-month-olds shown 4 toys from one category, each introduced with a novel noun, preferred an out-of-category toy at test. Those who heard no word did not ([Waxman & Markow 1995](https://doi.org/10.1006/cogp.1995.1016), from secondary summaries).
- **Novel nouns pull children from thematic to taxonomic choices.** Without a word, children picked the thematic match (duck → nest). Asked to "find another dax", they picked the same-kind match (duck → goose). One summary puts the shift at 25% to 65% taxonomic choices ([Markman & Hutchinson 1984](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(84)90002-1); lecture-slide figures, and I could not confirm which study or age group they come from). [Waxman & Gelman 1986](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0885-2014(86)80016-8) is widely cited for 3 and 4-year-olds sorting superordinate kinds better when a puppet gives each kind a foreign-language label. I did not read it.
- **The word need not be known.** In these studies the helpful labels were new words ("dax", Japanese). So "vehicles", a late word, can still work as the Bin's name.
- **Superordinate words act like collections.** Markman argued that young children treat words like "toys", "animals" and "furniture" as names for groups, not for single members. Many languages make superordinate terms mass nouns ("furniture", "food", "clothing") ([Markman 1985](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0277(85)90030-7); [Callanan & Markman 1982](https://doi.org/10.2307/1129151)). I read neither in full.
- **One exemplar invites matching by looks.** Toddlers generalise names by shape, and variable exemplars are what break that habit ([Perry et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3144952/)). A single dog on a Bin lets a child sort "four legs vs not" and still look correct on animals vs vehicles, though not on birds or fish.

**Design implication:** The Word is the category noun in its plural or mass form, said as the Thing lands: "Animals!", "Plants!", "Toys!", "Food!", "Vehicles!". The Bin shows 2 or 3 small, varied members, for example 🐕 🐦 🐟 for Animals. Don't show a place, and don't use only one exemplar. Tapping an empty Bin says its word.

## 4. Thematic vs taxonomic

- **No blanket thematic preference.** Children of 2 to 4 years, choosing between a thematic match (carrot → rabbit) and a same-kind match (carrot → tomato), showed "no pervasive preference" for either. Instructions, level and pictures vs objects shifted choices ([Waxman & Namy 1997](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.33.3.555), abstract).
- **But thematic pulls stay strong at 3 to 4.** 3-year-olds switched between thematic and taxonomic choices at random. Most 4-year-olds stuck with thematic matches, and only 5-year-olds switched on purpose when a scene cued it ([Blaye & Bonthoux 2001](https://lpc.univ-amu.fr/en/doc/hal-01441408/_1.html), abstract). In word association and picture matching, children aged 2;10 to 3;6 gave event-based relations, not slot-filler or taxonomic ones ([Sell 1992](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0305000900011612), abstract).
- **Places are scripts.** A barn cues farm animals. A kitchen cues food, spoons and pans. A house cues pets, toys and beds. Script categories such as breakfast foods and taxonomic ones such as fruit are both available at 3 (Nguyen & Murphy 2003), so a place picture gives the child a second, competing rule.

**Design implication:** Don't use place pictures on Bins. In early rounds, leave out Things with a strong theme link to the other Bin: bone, carrot or 🥚 in animals vs food; 🐶 or 🐱 in a home or toys round; 🍗, 🥩 or 🍣 in animals vs food; 🌽 or 🍎 in plants vs food. The Kinds Word on every landing is the counterweight (Markman & Hutchinson). Later, one theme-tempting Thing per round is a fair "tricky one".

## 5. Harder rounds (4 to 5)

- **Labels beat looks for natural kinds.** Young children use a shared category name over appearance for inferences. Two-year-olds did so in [Gelman & Coley 1990](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.26.5.796), whose title example is "a dodo is a bird". Four-year-olds did so in [Gelman & Markman 1986](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0277(86)90034-X). I read neither in full, so no numbers. This is the case for naming the category on a miss rather than explaining.
- **Life-forms are the easy finer split.** Dogs vs birds is separated in infancy (Mandler & McDonough 1998), so "birds" vs "animals with fur" is learnable. "Mammals" as a word is not needed. Birds, fish, land animals and bugs are the life-forms toddlers already respect.
- **Features.** I found no teaching study with numbers showing that calling out "feathers" or "fur" helps 4-year-olds sort mammals from birds. Treat a feature word as plausible but unproven.
- **Single-word clips limit reasons.** "A penguin is a bird" needs a sentence. With single clips the game can play item then category ("Penguin." … "Bird!"), which keeps the postnominal order the sister file recommends. It cannot give a reason such as "it has feathers" except as one more clip ("Feathers!"), which a 4-year-old may not link to the rule.

**Design implication:** In finer rounds, on a miss play the Thing's name, then its category word, and glow the right Bin. Atypical members appear only in these later rounds, at most 1 per round, always named that way. Don't add feature clips until a playtest shows they help.

## 6. Number of items, Bins and variety

- **Variety helps generalisation at no cost to the trained items.** 18-month-olds (n = 16) taught 12 categories learned trained labels equally well from tight or varied exemplars (63% vs 67%). Only the varied group generalised to new exemplars. The varied group's vocabulary grew by 9.78 words a week by the end of the study, against 4.03 for the tight group ([Perry et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3144952/)).
- **Too much variety can cost retention.** 2-year-olds who met several exemplars kept new words after a delay, and those who met one exemplar did not. But among the varied groups, only children who saw *narrow* sets kept the words. Those who saw broad sets did not ([Twomey, Ranson & Horst 2014](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/71108/), abstract).
- **Bins.** The superordinate evidence at 3 comes from 2-way contrasts (Nguyen & Murphy 2003), and Rosch's 3-choice triads with 2 kinds already dropped 3-year-olds to 55%.

**Design implication:** Start each Kind with typical, fairly similar members, such as farm and pet mammals for Animals. Widen within the same kind in later rounds: birds and fish join Animals. Use 2 Bins for the first two contrasts and 3 Bins only after both pairs are easy. Keep 8 Things a round as in the sister file, with no Thing repeated within a round.

## Addendum: follow-up numbers

A follow-up search, finished after the sections above were written, adds these. Full text was read unless marked.

- **Saying the group's name rescues odd members.** In [Gelman & Coley 1990](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/stuff_for_blog/dodo.pdf) (n=22, ages 2;1 to 3;1), 2-year-olds reasoned correctly about atypical animals (a dodo, a kiwi) on 69% of trials when the picture was named, and 42% when it wasn't, below chance. Typical members were about 75% either way. With names, 6 of 11 children reasoned by category and none by looks; without, 1 of 11 by category and 6 by looks. *Design implication:* in the Tricky Way the odd Thing must be named by its group, so a tap on it says its group's word, and the grown-up should say "a penguin is a bird".
- **Parts drive the youngest sorting.** In [Rakison & Butterworth 1998](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/infant-cognition-lab/publications/Rakison%20&%20Butterworth%20(1998a).pdf) (sequential touching, 16 per age at 14, 18 and 22 months), 78% of infants categorized normal animals vs vehicles, but only 58% when both had legs and wheels. When legs and wheels were swapped between kinds, 14 and 18-month-olds grouped by the part ("legs" vs "wheels"). *Design implication:* the first Step's pools should be animals with legs and vehicles with wheels; boats, planes, fish and snakes come later.
- **Food by group, at 3.** In [Nguyen & Murphy 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2442888/) (picture triads, chance 50%), 3-year-olds (n=16) were 60% right on taxonomic food triads: fruit .65, vegetables .43, bread .43, drinks .56, dairy .78, meat .75. 4-year-olds were 74% right.
- **Food vs non-food looks-alikes.** In [Foinant, Lafraire & Thibaut 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9533737/) (n=120 and 137, ages 4 to 6), children picked food over a look-alike non-food (a tomato and a red bauble) with 80 to 93% hits and 21 to 25% false alarms; sliced foods raised false alarms to 42%. *Design implication:* whole foods only, no slices.
- **Vegetables at 5.** In [Foinant et al. 2024](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Foinant_et_al._2024.pdf) (n=122, 57 to 75 months), children sorted vegetables from fruit and utensils at .84 mean accuracy, range .50 to 1, tied to vocabulary and executive function. Fruit and vegetables stays the late Step.
- **Food in its own colors.** [Macario 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0885-2014(91)90004-W) (abstract only): 3 and 4-year-olds used color to classify things they were told were food, and shape for toys. Emoji foods are in their usual colors already.
- **Plants and "alive".** In [Leddon, Waxman & Medin 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2659632/) (4 to 10 years), plants were counted as "alive" only at chance by 4 and 5-year-olds, but more often as "living things" (.54 vs .74 across ages). This confirms: never say "alive".
- **What counts as an animal.** Asked "X's are animals?", 5 and 6-year-olds (n=160) said yes to a cow .90 to .96, a bird .79 to .81, a fish .69 to .88, a bee .63 to .65, a worm .50 to .64, and a person only .03 to .13 ([Leddon et al., in press c.2012](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/medin/documents/Leddon_etal_inpress_Nova.pdf)). Three and 5-year-olds extended a name given to a dog and a bird to other animals, bee, ant, fish and snake included, and left people out ([Herrmann, Medin & Waxman 2012](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/medin/documents/HerrmannMedinWaxmanCognition2012.docx)). *Design implication:* no people and no insects in any animal group, as already planned.
- **One defining feature must be told.** Four and 5-year-olds (n=49) learned a category defined by one feature ("a short tail") only when told the rule; from examples alone they learned nothing. Categories whose members share many features were learned better from examples ([Kloos & Sloutsky 2008](https://artsci.uc.edu/content/dam/refresh/artsandsciences-62/labs/ccrl/docs/publications/Kloos_Sloutsky_2008a_accessible.pdf)). Asking 3 to 5-year-olds to explain pushed them to group by what things do rather than how they look ([Walker et al. 2014](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0cj544k3/qt0cj544k3.pdf), with toys, not animals). *Design implication:* Birds, Fish and Mammals are one-feature groups (feathers, fins, fur), so the rule has to be said. Single-word clips can't say it; the grown-up can, and the note should give it. Feature-word clips ("feathers") are untested.
- Allen 2015 (n=75, ages 3 to 5, labels animal, fish, bird, mammal, insect) is the best-targeted study for whale, penguin and insect items, but only its abstract was read; a secondary summary says 5-year-olds did worse than younger children on atypical members.
- **A puppet's bins, named, at 3: nearly perfect.** In [Waxman & Gelman 1986](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/waxman/documents/WaxmanGelman1986CogDev.pdf), children sorted 12 drawings into a puppet's bins for animals, clothing and food (12 perfect, chance 6). Three-year-olds scored 11.39 when told the group's plural name ("this puppet only likes animals"), 9.00 when shown instances ("things like a dog, or a horse, or a duck") and 9.72 with instances and "a good group"; a replication gave 11.25, 8.81 and 9.33. Four-year-olds were near 11.5 in every condition. Plural Japanese words the children didn't know worked too (10.53). By group, 3-year-olds did food 10.19, clothing 9.92, animals 9.28. *Design implication:* this is the closest study to Kinds. Animals, clothes and food is an early three-Bin Step, and the Bins' plural Words matter more than their pictures, so each Bin says its Word as the Round starts.
- **The group words by age.** From Wordbank's CDI norms (US English, children producing the word; re-norming n=2169): "toy" 66% at 24 months and 93% at 30; "food" 58% and 90%; "animal" 48% and 81%; "plant" 33% and 71%. "Clothes", "fruit", "vegetable" and "vehicle" aren't on the form. Adults' estimates of age of acquisition ([Kuperman et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-012-0210-4), read from a mirror): animal 2.9 years, toy 3.0, clothes 3.1, food 3.3, fruit 3.6, plant 4.0, vegetable 4.2, furniture 5.9.
- **Two pictures beat one.** Three-year-olds extended a new group word only when shown two 3-D examples; 4 and 5-year-olds managed with two pictures or one object ([Liu, Golinkoff & Sak 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11768139/), abstract only). Supports several small pictures on a Bin rather than one.
- **A word lifts group choices from chance.** Two-year-olds (mean 2;7) chose the same-group picture .72 of the time with a new noun, .56 with an adjective and .51 with no word ([Waxman & Kosowski 1990](https://doi.org/10.2307/1130756)). In Markman & Hutchinson 1984, 4 and 5-year-olds made group choices over "goes with" choices 25% of the time with no word and 65% with one (figures from Markman 1990, a secondary source by the first author).
- **How parents say group words.** In CHILDES speech to 597 children aged 1 to 5, group words ("toy", "animal", "fruit") came far more often than matched basic nouns in the plural, after "some"/"all", and after "kind of" (about 5 times as likely), and less often in "this is a…" ([Choe & Papafragou 2025](https://yjunechoe.github.io/static/papers/Choe_Papafragou_2025_distributional_signatures_superordinates.pdf)). The counts: "toy" 3,317, "food" 3,081, "animal" 2,829, "clothes" 1,255, "furniture" 73, "vehicle" 21. When parents taught group words, they anchored them to basic names and used them for groups of things ([Callanan 1985](https://doi.org/10.2307/1129738), abstract and snippets). Three and 5-year-olds read a word as a group word when it labelled several things at once, or came with "X is a kind of Y" ([Callanan 1989](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.25.2.207), abstract). *Design implication:* the plural Word said over a crate of several things is how children hear these words anyway. "Vehicles" is almost never said to children; Waxman & Gelman's unknown plural words still worked, so keep it, and the note tells the grown-up to join the words: "a dog is an animal", "all of these are animals".
- **Group words by difficulty, 3 to 5.** Children (n=75, 25 each at 3, 4 and 5) sorted plastic models by label; mean accuracy was animal 84%, fish 72%, bird 68%, insect 61%, mammal 48%. Big four-legged mammals were right at every age; 5-year-olds did worse than 3-year-olds on odd members, such as calling a dolphin a fish ([Allen 2015](https://researchinnovation.kingston.ac.uk/files/48082799/Allen-M-27578.docx), preprint read, item tables missing). *Design implication:* "mammal" is the hardest label of all, so Mammals stays the last Step.
- **Odd members, kindergarten.** Five and 6-year-olds (n=24) counted a penguin as a bird 71% of the time, a tomato as a fruit 42%, a mushroom as a vegetable 67%, a tricycle as a vehicle 75%; typical members were near 100% ([Bjorklund, Thompson & Ornstein 1983](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03203657)). Children of 9 to 11 still made 23% errors verifying peripheral members ("a chicken is a bird") against 6% for central ones ([Rosch 1973](https://www.academia.edu/24474386), tables read). *Design implication:* Tricky's odd members will often be missed at first, which is the point; the Miss help and the grown-up carry them.
- Numbers for Mervis & Pani 1980, Anglin 1977 and Carey 1985 could not be checked; Carey's human-animal pattern was replicated in 5-year-olds (Herrmann, Waxman & Medin 2010). A search snippet giving 3 to 4-year-olds 80% hits and 50% false alarms on food vs non-food (attributed to Rioux, Picard & Lafraire 2016) could not be traced to its source; it is left out.

## Recommendations for the spec

1. **Kinds ladder** (one Scope per step, 2 Bins unless noted):
   1. Animals vs Vehicles.
   2. Animals vs Plants (trees, flowers, cactus, potted plant).
   3. Toys vs Clothes.
   4. Animals / Vehicles / Plants (3 Bins).
   5. Food vs Toys.
   6. Animals vs Birds (typical birds only; the "Animals" Bin shows furry animals).
   7. Animals vs Fish.
   8. Fruit vs Vegetables (textbook members only).
   9. Mixed "tricky one" rounds that add one atypical member.
2. **Drop "home things" as a Bin.** It is a place, not a kind, and it cues thematic sorting. Use "Toys" and "Clothes" instead. "Furniture" is a later Scope at most.
3. **Plants** are an early Bin as a looks-alike kind. Never pair Plants with Food, and never say "alive".
4. **Bin picture:** 2 or 3 small, varied members of the kind, taken from outside the round's Pile. No place scenes and no single exemplar.
5. **The Word** is the kind's plural or mass noun: "Animals", "Vehicles", "Plants", "Toys", "Clothes", "Food", "Birds", "Fish", "Fruit", "Vegetables". It plays on each landing and when the empty Bin is tapped. "Vehicles" is fine even if the child doesn't know it.
6. **On a miss**, play the Thing's name and then the right kind's word ("Penguin." … "Bird!"). Never say the wrong Bin's word.
7. **Exclude early** (steps 1 to 5): penguin, ostrich, bat, whale, dolphin, seal, butterfly, bee, ladybird, snail, worm, dinosaurs, dragon, unicorn, people, teddy bear 🧸, mushroom 🍄, tomato 🍅, avocado 🥑, corn 🌽, peppers 🌶️ 🫑, nuts, egg 🥚, meat and fish dishes 🍗 🥩 🍣, toy-like vehicles 🏎️ 🛝, and any toy that is an animal or vehicle.
8. **Emoji choice:** use full-body animal emoji (🐕 🐈 🐄 🐖 🐑 🐎), not face-only emoji (🐶 🐱 🐮). Full bodies are closer to the models and photos in the studies and show legs, feathers and fins. In any round with a Toys Bin, drop all vehicles, because a picture of a car can be a toy car or a car. Prefer Noto or Apple glyphs that look realistic over cartoony ones.
9. **Variety:** each round draws 8 Things from a pool of about 12 per kind, so rounds differ. Early steps use a narrow pool (mammals only for Animals). Later steps widen it.
10. **Advance** after a round with at most 1 hint, the same rule as colors. The grown-up picks the start step on the first screen.

## Open questions / weak evidence

- **Paywalled core papers.** I read only abstracts or secondary summaries of Mandler & Bauer 1988, Mandler et al. 1991, Mandler & McDonough 1993, Markman & Hutchinson 1984, Waxman & Gelman 1986, Waxman & Markow 1995, Callanan & Markman 1982, Markman 1985, Gelman & Coley 1990, Gelman & Markman 1986, Mervis & Pani 1980 and Opfer & Siegler 2004. The Markman & Hutchinson 25% to 65% figures come from lecture slides and are not tied to an age group.
- **Word age of acquisition.** I did not get CDI or Wordbank data on when "animal", "toy", "clothes", "food", "plant" and "vehicle" are understood. The order of steps 3 and 5 rests on reasoning, not data.
- **Rosch's 55% at 3** came from 10 children with no feedback. A game with a Word and bump-back feedback may do better. Watch the playtest.
- **Feature words** ("feathers", "fur") have no direct evidence at this age.
- **Exemplar pictures on Bins** vs a word alone has not been tested with toddlers on touch screens. It is a judgment call from the shape-bias and variability studies.
- **Atypical members and insects:** I found no numbers for 3 to 4-year-olds on penguin, whale or bee specifically. The exclusion list rests on typicality theory and adult intuition.
