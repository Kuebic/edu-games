# Toddler sorting game: research for a color and shape sorting Game, ages 2 to 3

Researched 2026-09-28 for a new Game on the shelf: objects appear one at a time and the child moves each into the bin that matches its color, later its shape.

## Summary

Sort by color first and by shape later, never both in one round. Start with 2 bins. In the first Levels every object is the same toy in a different color, because toddlers match identical objects by color long before they can match a red cup with a red duck. Drag works for most 3-year-olds on a phone, but 2-year-olds struggle to finish a drop, so use big bin zones and let a tap on a bin place the object too. A wrong bin never buzzes. The object slides back, the Voice names its real color, and the right bin glows. After each correct placement the Voice says "The duck is red", object first and color last. The reward is the full bins, with no stickers or points. Many 3-year-olds already know nine colors, so the Levels must climb fast. Pick color pairs that red-green color-blind children can tell apart, and put a pattern cue behind a Grown-up Corner switch.

## 1. Developmental readiness

- **Grouping into 2 classes starts early.** Children of 12 to 24 months already group or touch objects of one class in sequence when given 4 + 4 objects that differ in color, size or shape ([Ricciuti 1965, Sugarman 1981, reviewed in Alessandroni & Rodriguez 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7377002/)). All of these studies used 2 classes.
- **Under 4, children prefer color over form.** Asked to "point to the two that are the same", children of 2.8 to 3.9 years chose by color, children of 3.9 to 4.5 showed no preference, and children of 4.5 to 5.5 chose by form ([Suchman & Trabasso 1966](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0022096566900920)).
- **Matching by color depends on overall similarity.** Two-year-olds match same-colored objects when the objects are identical or nearly so. They fail when the objects differ, for example a red cup and a red cat. In a longitudinal study of 2-year-olds, matching dissimilar objects by color came *after* children understood color words ([Sandhofer & Smith 1999](https://babytalk.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Sandhofer_Smith_1999.pdf)). Size matching came earlier than color matching. [Soja 1994](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-8624.1994.tb00793.x) found 2-year-olds could reason from color without color words, so the studies disagree at the edges. Both find identical objects the easy case.
- **Comprehension precedes naming.** Children first overextend color words: "red" covers red plus its neighbours ([Wagner, Dobkins & Barner 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23542408/)). In a 1-of-3 color pointing task plus a naming task, children reached reliable knowledge of nine basic colors within a 3-month window, 35.6 to 39.5 months of language age. Yellow (35.6) and blue (36.4) came first and red came at 39.2. Brown and grey came 6 to 9 months later. There was little support for a fixed "primary colors first" order ([Pitchford & Mullen 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12489771/)). The CDC lists "Names a few colors of items" as a 4-year milestone, meaning 75% or more of children do it by 4 ([CDC 4 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html), [Zubler et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2021-052138)).
- **Shape names come around 30 months.** 25-month-olds knew few shape names, and triangles and rectangles were not above chance. By 30 months children knew most shape names, and circle and square scored better than rectangle. Star was the best-known shape at both ages. Typical shapes were easier than shapes embedded in pictures ([Verdine et al. 2016](https://sites.temple.edu/newcombe/files/2013/06/The-Shape-of-Things.pdf)).
- **3-year-olds cannot switch sorting rules.** In the Dimensional Change Card Sort, 3-year-olds sort correctly by the first rule, then keep using it after being told to switch. Most 5-year-olds switch ([Zelazo 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17406248/)). A meta-analysis of 69 reports found that switching improves when the new dimension is more salient, when the dimensions are spatially separated, and when the child labels the stimuli ([Doebel & Zelazo 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26955206/)).

**Design implication:** Teach color before shape. Start with identical objects that differ only in color, then vary the objects. Use 2 bins and add a third later. For shape Levels, make every object the same color. Change the rule only between rounds, with a new-looking scene.

## 2. Touch input

- **Drag is harder than tap, and the gap closes with age.** 89 children aged 3 to 6 reached 98.7% completion on tap and 92.0% on single-finger drag-and-drop, but only 53.7% on two-finger drag. The under-4 group had the lowest overall completion, 73.5%. Children dragged better on a 3.5-inch phone than on a 10-inch tablet, with drag failures of 4.2% versus 11.5% of attempts. Failed drags came from fingers briefly lifting off the screen ([Vatavu, Cramariuc & Schipor 2015](https://mintviz.usv.ro/publications/ijhcs2015.pdf)).
- The same study measured tap offsets of 4.5 mm for 3-year-olds, against 3.4 mm for children over 5. Taps by children under 4 lasted up to 5.1 s. The authors recommend accepting 10 mm offsets and 5 s taps, and treating several fingers on a dragged object like one.
- **2-year-olds struggle with drag-and-drop.** A high share of 2-year-olds failed drag-and-drop, free rotate, pinch and spread, while 4-year-olds managed all seven gestures ([Abdul Aziz et al. 2014](https://thesai.org/Publications/ViewPaper?Volume=5&Issue=4&Code=IJACSA&SerialNo=15)). The TIDRC review turns this into "avoid drag & drop under 4" ([Soni et al. 2019](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/378/2019/04/TIDRC-Framework-soni-et-al-IDC19-final.pdf)). Vatavu's data and the playtest, where the 3-year-old loved dragging cars in Traffic Jam, suggest that is too strict for 3-year-olds.
- Sesame Workshop, drawing on 50+ touch studies: children can drag but "have difficulty with finger-on-screen continuity", so support partial completion. Register input on touch, not on lift. Expect unintended multi-touch. Keep hot spots large and well apart. Children rest their wrists on a tablet's bottom edge ([Sesame Workshop 2012](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/SesameWorkshop-2012.pdf)).
- **Dragging may help learning.** Among 170 children aged 2 to 4, dragging a named object helped some groups learn labels more than tapping did: girls over 2 and low-SES children. Boys learned more from watching. Tapping is "a prepotent response that does not require thoughtful attention" ([Russo-Johnson et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00578)).
- Platform minimums, 44 pt on iOS ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/layout)) and 48 dp on Android ([Android](https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/ui/accessibility/apps)), are about 7 to 9 mm and made for adults.
- In one Indian clinic sample only 27% of 2 to 3-year-olds tapped an intended spot ([Yadav et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7303424/)), against over 90% of 2-year-olds showing moderate tablet skill in YouTube videos ([Hourcade et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1145/2702123.2702266)). Device exposure probably explains the gap.

**Design implication:** Offer drag, and let a tap on a bin do the same job. Use big targets, short drags and generous drop zones. A lifted finger leaves the object where it is so the child can pick it up again. Track one pointer and ignore the rest.

## 3. Feedback and errors

- Sesame scaffolds wrong answers in 3 steps: name the error and encourage, then restate the goal with a hint, then highlight the right answer and repeat until the child gets it. Idle time-outs come after 6 to 8 s in games ([Sesame Workshop 2012](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/SesameWorkshop-2012.pdf)).
- **Corrective contrast teaches color words.** 3-year-olds taught new color terms by corrective contrast ("that's not X, it's Y") learned faster and more than with other kinds of contrast ([O'Hanlon & Roberson 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16438979/)). Contrasting a new term with the child's own label helped 3 and 4-year-olds when repeated ([Au & Laframboise 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2083500/)).
- Errorless learning research is mostly about children with developmental disorders ([Mueller et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1002/pits.20258)). I found no touch-screen study of it in typical toddlers.
- 2 to 3-year-olds could not follow in-app prompts such as animated hands, audio or video. Animation worked best from age 4 ([Yadav et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7303424/)). 2.5 and 3-year-olds did not learn a touch-screen puzzle from a "ghost" demonstration where pieces moved by themselves, but did learn from a person demonstrating ([Zimmermann et al. 2017](https://elp.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Zimmermann_et_al-2016-Child_Development.pdf)).

**Design implication:** Use no buzzer, no lives and no fail state. On a wrong bin the object slides back, the Voice names its color, and the right bin glows. On a second miss the object goes into the right bin on its own. Give the idle hint at about 7 s. Don't count on animated-hand tutorials.

## 4. Voice and audio

- **Postnominal color words teach better.** 2-year-olds trained with postnominal color phrases such as "the crayon is yellow" improved over baseline. Those trained with prenominal phrases such as "the yellow crayon" did not ([Ramscar et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2009.01092.x), one lab, small samples).
- The CDC's tip for 2-year-olds: do simple shape and color puzzles and "name each piece when your child puts it in place" ([CDC 2 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html)).
- For the youngest toddlers of 24 to 36 months, word learning from a touch screen improved only when the child had to touch the specific labeled object. The same condition hurt slightly older children ([Kirkorian, Choi & Pempek 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12508); similar for object search in [Choi & Kirkorian 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616636110)).
- Sesame: keep speech short with the key word last, pair it with a highlight, and let children interrupt non-essential prompts ([2012](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/SesameWorkshop-2012.pdf)).

**Design implication:** After each correct placement, play one short recorded line with the object first and the color last: "The duck is red." Different objects keep the line from repeating exactly. Don't read out an instruction before every item. Tapping a bin plays its color word.

## 5. Session length, pacing and variety

- Focused attention during free play grows across ages 1 to 4.5 ([Ruff & Lawson 1990](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.26.1.85)). I found no primary source for the popular "2 to 3 minutes per year of age" rule. Treat it as folklore.
- Repetition helps learning. 3-year-olds who heard the *same* story three times learned and retained new words, while those who heard different stories with the same words did not ([Horst et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00017)).
- The playtest found repetition bores this 3-year-old, and she outgrows scaffolds fast. Pitchford & Mullen's data suggest a typical 3-year-old may already know red, blue, yellow and green.

**Design implication:** Keep the frame the same (bins, gesture, Voice line) and vary the content (objects, colors, bin count). Use rounds of 8 objects, about 1 to 2 minutes each. Move up a Level quickly.

## 6. Rewards

- Preschoolers who expected a reward for drawing later drew less on their own ([Lepper, Greene & Nisbett 1973](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0035519)). 20-month-olds given material rewards for helping later helped less. Social praise did not have this effect ([Warneken & Tomasello 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0013860)).
- In e-books, animation, music and sound effects helped comprehension, while hotspots and games distracted, especially for disadvantaged children ([Takacs, Swart & Bus 2015](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314566989)). The AAP advises avoiding "apps with lots of distracting content" ([AAP 2016](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2016-2591)).
- Sesame: payoffs should reflect the concept and the child's choice. TIDRC: avoid points and progress bars for ages 4 and under ([Soni et al. 2019](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/378/2019/04/TIDRC-Framework-soni-et-al-IDC19-final.pdf)).

**Design implication:** Make the reward the finished sort. The full bins wobble and the red things parade, with a Sound. This confirms the shelf's earlier "reward shows the content" rule. Use no stickers, points, stars or progress bars.

## 7. Learning from screens and transfer

- Across 59 reports, children aged 0 to 6 learn about half a standard deviation less from video than from live teaching. The deficit shrinks with age ([Strouse & Samson 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13429)). Across 36 studies, touch screens produced learning of d = 0.46 in 0 to 5-year-olds ([Xie et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02580)).
- A two-way transfer deficit between a touch-screen puzzle and a real puzzle persisted at 3 years and was larger at 2.5 ([Moser et al. 2015](https://elp.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/JECPMoser-et-al-2015.pdf)).
- Infants aged 15 months were 19 times more likely to transfer between a touch screen and a real toy when their mother's interaction was high quality ([Zack & Barr 2016](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01264)).
- 15 and 18-month-olds carried labels between pictures and real objects more often with realistic photos and drawings than with cartoons ([Ganea et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/15248370701836592)). 18-month-olds taught with varied exemplars generalized to new ones, while those taught with near-identical exemplars did not ([Perry et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610389189)).
- AAP: for ages 2 to 5, 1 hour a day of high-quality media, co-viewed ([AAP 2016](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/10/161021121843.htm)). WHO: under 1 hour of sedentary screen time for ages 2 to 4, "less is better" ([WHO 2019](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more)). The CDC suggests asking a 3-year-old to find objects that are the same around the house ([CDC 3 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html)).

**Design implication:** Draw realistic everyday objects such as a duck, apple, sock, cup or car. Use several different objects per color once the identical-object Levels are done. End each round with a prompt a grown-up can read: "Find something red!"

## 8. Accessibility: color vision deficiency

- About 1 in 12 men have color vision deficiency. Red-green is most common. Blue-yellow types, rarer, confuse blue with green and yellow with red or pink ([NEI](https://www.nei.nih.gov/learn-about-eye-health/eye-conditions-and-diseases/color-blindness/types-color-vision-deficiency)). WCAG 1.4.1 says color must not be the only means of conveying information ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)).
- The tension is real. If objects and bins share a pattern, the child can sort by pattern and never look at color. An icon on the bin alone gives nothing away, but it also doesn't help a color-blind child, who still can't see the object's color.

**Design implication:** Choose pairs that red-green color-blind children can separate: red/blue and yellow/blue. Never make red/green, or blue/green in early Levels, the only pair. Give each color a clearly different lightness. Put patterns on object and bin behind a Grown-up Corner switch, off by default. The Voice names the color when the child touches the object, which helps every child.

## Recommendations for the spec

1. Color Levels come first and shape Levels after. A round never mixes rules, and a shape round looks different from a color round, with a shape-hole box instead of colored buckets.
2. Level 1 has 2 bins, red and blue, and 8 copies of one identical block, 4 per color.
3. Ladder: red/blue blocks, then yellow/blue blocks, then red/blue/yellow with 3 bins, then varied toys of the same kind, then mixed realistic everyday objects, then add green, orange, purple and pink. Brown and grey come last or not at all.
4. Shape Levels: circle/square first, then add triangle. All objects share one neutral color. Star is an optional bonus shape. Rectangle comes last.
5. Advance a Level after a round with at most 1 hint. The grown-up can pick the starting Level with one tap on the first screen.
6. Objects are at least 20 mm across, about 125 CSS px on a phone. The grab area extends 10 mm, about 60 px, beyond the drawing.
7. Split the screen into bin zones. Releasing the object anywhere in a bin's zone drops it there, and it snaps into the bin. The object starts 25 to 40 mm from the bins.
8. A tap on a bin sends the waiting object there. This works at every Level, with no setting.
9. Register grabs on pointer down. The first pointer that lands on the object owns the drag. Ignore other pointers until it lifts. Extra fingers or a palm on the object count as that same grab. A press of up to 5 s is still a tap.
10. If the finger lifts outside any bin, the object stays where it was dropped, can be grabbed again, and pulses after 7 s idle.
11. Wrong bin: a soft bump Sound, no buzzer. The object slides back. The Voice says "That's blue" and the blue bin glows. On a second miss with the same object, the object glides into the right bin by itself while the Voice says "Blue."
12. Idle 7 s: the right bin glows and the Voice says its color. Idle again: the object moves itself. No tutorial hands.
13. Correct placement: a Sound, plus a recorded Voice line with the object first and the color last ("The duck is red"). Speak the round instruction only on the first object. Recorded clips will be needed, one per object-color pair used.
14. A round is 8 objects, or 9 with 3 bins. Play a fixed session of 3 rounds, then a calm end screen.
15. The reward is the full bins animating, with no stickers, points, stars or progress bar.
16. Every round ends with a card the grown-up reads aloud: "Find something red!"
17. Add a Grown-up Corner switch for Patterns, off by default, that puts matching patterns on objects and bins for color-blind children.

## Open questions and weak evidence

- **Drag for 2-year-olds.** Abdul Aziz found 2-year-olds struggle with drag and gave no percentages. Vatavu tested no one under 3. Watch the first playtest to see whether the tap-a-bin fallback gets used.
- **Minutes of attention.** I found no reliable number for 2 to 3-year-olds. Round and session length here are judgment calls, to tune by playtest.
- **2 versus 3 bins.** No study directly compares bin counts at this age. Classic sorting studies all use 2 classes, and Pitchford's 1-of-3 color task worked from about 36 months.
- **Postnominal phrasing** rests on one lab. It costs nothing to adopt.
- **Errorless versus corrective feedback** has no touch-screen study in typical toddlers. The recommendation combines Sesame's practice with the corrective-contrast findings.
- **Touch contingency cuts both ways.** It helped younger toddlers and hurt slightly older ones ([Kirkorian 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12508); [Choi et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.621372)). Effects of drag versus tap differed by sex and SES.
- **Pattern cue** is unresolved. Default-off is a guess, and color vision deficiency is rarely diagnosed at 2 to 3.
- **Paywalls.** I read only abstracts or summaries of the AAP policy and of Nacher et al. 2015 on 2 to 3-year-olds' gestures. The AAP "distracting content" wording comes from summaries.
