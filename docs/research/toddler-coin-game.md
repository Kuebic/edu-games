# Toddler coin game: research for a US coin and dollar Game, ages 2 to 4

Researched 2026-09-28 for a new Game on the shelf: the child meets pennies, nickels, dimes, quarters and a dollar bill on a phone and learns their names. Drag, feedback, rewards, session length and learning from screens are covered in [toddler-sorting-game.md](./toddler-sorting-game.md) and built into Tidy Up. This doc cites them only where coins change the answer.

## Summary

Teach names, not values. The one study with 2 and 3-year-olds found that most 3-year-olds name the penny, some name the others, and children under 4 tell coins apart by size and brightness. Start with the penny and the quarter, which differ in both color and size. Add the dime next, the smallest coin, then the dollar bill, and the nickel last, because children still mix up the nickel and the quarter at 6. Show 2 kinds at once and go to 3 later. Never show more than 3. The main task is sorting coins into piggy banks, each bank showing a picture of its coin. That is identical-picture matching, and the child drags, which the user's 3-year-old loves. A second mode asks "Find the dime!" with 2 or 3 coins to pick from and names the coin again after the tap. Use realistic photos of the heads side at true relative size, scaled up so the dime is still 20 mm across. Tails comes later, as a flip. The reward is the banks filling up with a clink, and the full bank wobbling at the end. Leave out values, cents, the ¢ sign, "which is worth more", counting mixed coins, making change, the dollar coin, the half-dollar and the 2026 one-year designs. A pretend shop where one coin buys one thing fits a 4-year-old. A shop with prices does not fit until about 6. The Game also means a child can handle coins with no choking risk, since coins are the object young children swallow most. Play with real coins stays a supervised job for a grown-up.

## 1. When children learn coin names, and which ones they mix up

- **The only direct data are old and small.** Edmunds & Whitehurst interviewed 80 Black children aged 2 to 8 in Virginia, 8 two-year-olds and 12 per age after that, half middle and half lower SES ([Edmunds & Whitehurst c. 1973, ERIC ED087553](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED087553.pdf)). I found no newer study that tested US coin naming in 2 to 4-year-olds.
- **2-year-olds know it's money but not which.** They called every coin "money", or used one name such as "penny" or "nickel" for all of them. Some already called all paper money "dollar", and calling the $1 and $5 bills both "dollar" lasted until 7.
- **By 3 most name the penny.** "By the age of three a majority of the children recognized the penny and some of them correctly identified the nickel, dime and quarter." A majority named the penny at 3, the dime at 5, and the nickel, quarter, $1 and $5 bills at 6. The half-dollar came at 8.
- **Big nickel, little nickel.** 3-year-olds used one name plus "big" or "little": if every coin was a "nickel", a quarter was a "big nickel" and a dime a "little nickel". Size is the first cue they reach for.
- **A dip at 4.** Recognition of the nickel, dime and quarter *fell* at 4, and 4-year-olds confused the three. The authors guess that under-4s used "size and brightness cues" and that at 4 they moved off those cues. 5-year-olds knew the penny and dime but "were still confusing the nickel and the quarter", and so did some 6-year-olds.
- **Experience drives it.** Lower-SES children named coins earlier and middle-SES children named bills earlier. The authors put this down to which money each group handled. In 2024 cash was 14% of US consumer payments ([Federal Reserve 2025 Diary](https://www.frbservices.org/news/research/2025-findings-from-the-diary-of-consumer-payment-choice)), so a 3-year-old today may handle fewer coins than the 1970s sample did.
- **The physical cues.** The penny is the only copper-colored coin. The dime is the smallest at 17.91 mm, then the penny at 19.05, the nickel at 21.21 and the quarter at 24.26. The penny and nickel have plain edges and the dime and quarter reeded ones ([coin table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_the_United_States_dollar)). Nickel and quarter are both silver and only 3 mm apart, which fits Edmunds' finding that they stay confused longest.
- **Adults don't use the pictures either.** Adults were poor at drawing a penny or picking the right drawing out of a set. People store only enough detail to tell a coin from the other coins ([Nickerson & Adams 1979](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(79)90013-6)).

**Design implication:** Order the coins by how far apart they look. Penny and quarter differ in color and size, so they come first. Penny and dime differ in color only, and dime and quarter in size only. Nickel against quarter is the hardest pair and comes last. Keep the true colors and relative sizes, because those are the cues a 3-year-old uses.

## 2. Name versus value, and what the standards expect

- **Before 4, "more" doesn't mean value.** In Edmunds & Whitehurst, children under 4 could not do the "which is more?" task at all. From about 4, "more" meant the bigger coin, and choosing by size faded slowly through 7. Most children picked the coin worth more only at 6, and all of them did at 8. A dime worth more than a nickel is a 6-year-old's idea.
- **Common Core starts money in grade 2.** 2.MD.C.8: "Solve word problems involving dollar bills, quarters, dimes, nickels, and pennies, using $ and ¢ symbols appropriately" ([CCSS 2.MD.C.8](https://thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/2/MD/C/8/)). Common Core has no coin standard in K or grade 1.
- **Some states name coins in kindergarten or grade 1.** Texas kindergarten: "identify U.S. coins by name, including pennies, nickels, dimes, and quarters" ([TEKS §111.2 K.4(A)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-111-2)). Florida grade 1 adds values and the ¢ sign, and its Clarification 1 says "Instruction includes the recognition of both sides of a coin" ([CPALMS MA.1.M.2.2](https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/Export/15272)).
- **Preschool standards ask for money as a thing, and sorting.** Colorado's preschool indicators, by about 5 years: "Recognize coins and currency as money", "Identify how money is used", and "Sort coins by physical attributes such as color or size". Its examples for adults are pretend play with play money, a shop, a bank or a restaurant, and "Use names of coins and currency when talking about money" ([Colorado 2020 PFL standards](https://www.cde.state.co.us/cofinancialliteracy/pflstandards)).
- **Head Start says nothing about money.** I searched the full Head Start Early Learning Outcomes Framework and found no mention of money, coins or dollars. Its toddler cognition goal for 16 to 36 months is "Sorts objects into two groups based on a single characteristic" ([ELOF 2015](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-HE23_1100-PURL-gpo65456/pdf/GOVPUB-HE23_1100-PURL-gpo65456.pdf)). I did not check NCTM's pre-K Focal Points.
- **Practice guidance agrees.** "Most preschoolers do not yet understand the values and uses of money." The Illinois tip sheet suggests sorting, stacking, rubbing and looking at coins with a magnifier ([Illinois Early Learning, Math Sense: Learning about Coins](https://illinoisearlylearning.org/tipsheets/coins/)).

**Design implication:** Teach 2 to 4-year-olds that coins are money, that each coin has a name, and that coins sort by look. Leave out values, cents, ¢ and "worth more". A dime is not "the little one that's worth more" at this age, just "the dime".

## 3. Money and buying

- **Stages.** Berti & Bombi interviewed children aged 3 to 8. In their early stages children know payment happens but see no difference between kinds of money, and any money buys anything. Only later do they learn that money can be too little and that the amount must match the price ([Berti & Bombi 1988](https://libcat.library.tamu.edu/Record/in00000034283); I read only secondary summaries).
- Edmunds & Whitehurst saw the same thing. At 2, children knew money "had something to do with buying; however, any denomination of money could buy any object". A few 4-year-olds matched coins to things they could buy. Most children did so at 6 or 7.
- In Strauss's interviews of children from about 4 up, the youngest gave "four coins for four objects, or two coins for a small candy and four for a large". They could recognize coins long before they could make change ([Strauss 1952](https://doi.org/10.2307/2088073), via [a summary](https://howardsbecker.com/articles/money.html)).
- **Counting has its own timeline.** Children learn what "three" means as a set size, the cardinal principle, over several months between about 3 and 4 ([Sarnecka & Carey 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2008.05.007)). "Pay 3 pennies" is a counting task first, and only a money task after.
- **What grown-up guides recommend for 3 to 5.** The CFPB's Money as You Grow sheet for early childhood is mostly about executive function: waiting, planning, taking turns. On coins it suggests "games or activities with coins in which they match, sort, and count coins", and pretend play about "going to work, getting paid, depositing and withdrawing money from the bank, shopping, and saving money" ([CFPB 2019](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_parents_early-childhood-handout.pdf)). Sesame Workshop's *For Me, For You, For Later* kit, from 2011, has spend, share and save jars, a pretend store, and "sort and count the money his store 'earns'". It barely mentions coin names ([Sesame Workshop 2011](https://sesameworkshop.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Finance_CAB_ENG_F.pdf)).
- A Cambridge literature review for the UK Money Advice Service is often cited as saying money habits form by age 7 ([Whitebread & Bingham 2013](https://maps.org.uk/content/dam/maps-corporate/en/our-work/mas-habit-formation-and-learning-in-young-children-may-2013.pdf)). I could not open the PDF and read only press summaries. None of these guides reports a tested effect on 3-year-olds.

**Design implication:** A piggy bank that coins go into is honest at this age. It is where money goes, and it needs no values. A shop fits later, for about 4 up, if one coin buys one thing and the coin doesn't matter. That is the stage the child is at anyway. A shop with prices, or "which coin buys the apple", waits until 6. Saving as waiting for something is a grown-up conversation, not a game mechanic.

## 4. Find the named coin, or match the same coin

- **Understanding a word comes before saying it.** The PPVT, the standard receptive vocabulary test, starts at 2 years 6 months and asks the child to point to one of 4 pictures ([PPVT-5](https://www.pearsonclinical.com.au/en-au/Store/Professional-Assessments/Speech-&-Language/Literacy/Peabody-Picture-Vocabulary-Test,-5th-Edition/p/P100010207)). That is a test, though, not a lesson.
- **Picking is easy and remembering is hard.** 24-month-olds were "excellent at referent selection, but very poor at retention" of new names after 5 minutes. They kept the first 4 names when the experimenter named the object again after the child had picked it ([Horst & Samuelson 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/15250000701795598)).
- **Fewer choices, better memory.** 30-month-olds picked a named new object from among 2, 3 or 4 competitors equally well. Only those who saw few competitors remembered the names later ([Horst, Scott & Pollard 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-7687.2009.00926.x)).
- **Matching identical things comes before words.** For colors, 2-year-olds matched identical objects before they knew color words, and matched different objects only after ([Sandhofer & Smith 1999](https://babytalk.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Sandhofer_Smith_1999.pdf), see the sorting doc). A penny dropped into a bank with a penny on the front is that easy kind of match, the same as Tidy Up's Blocks.
- The sorting doc covers the rest. Put the key word last, give one short line, and use touch contingency, which helps the youngest ([Sesame Workshop 2012](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/SesameWorkshop-2012.pdf); [Kirkorian et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12508)).

**Design implication:** Sorting into piggy banks by picture is the first task. It needs no words, and the Word clip on landing teaches the name. Find the named coin is the second task. Offer 2 coins, then 3, and after a correct tap play the name again: "Dime!" The coin name is the word the child hears last. The user can't record, so every coin needs a recorded Word clip, as in Tidy Up.

## 5. Pictures, sides and size on screen

- **Realistic pictures transfer better.** 15 and 18-month-olds carried a label from a picture to the real object more often with realistic photos than cartoons ([Ganea et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/15248370701836592)). 18-month-olds learned more from color photos than drawings, and the gap closed by 30 months ([Simcock & DeLoache 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.42.6.1352)). A coin is flat and gray, so a clean photo is also easier to draw than a cartoon.
- **US coin photos are free.** Commons marks US Mint coin images as a work of the US Government, in the public domain ([example](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:2005_Penny_Obv_Unc_D.png)).
- **Both sides.** No study I found tests heads versus tails with young children. Florida teaches both sides in grade 1. Varied examples help 18-month-olds generalize ([Perry et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610389189)). Tails is where the designs vary most (section 6), so it is the harder side.
- **True size.** Nothing tests on-screen coin size either. Edmunds' under-4s used size, so drawing the coins at their true ratios keeps that cue. At the sorting doc's 6.25 CSS px per mm, true size is dime 112 px, penny 119, nickel 133 and quarter 152. The dime is below the 20 mm minimum for a toddler's target. Scaling everything by 1.15 keeps the ratios and gives dime 129 px, penny 137, nickel 153 and quarter 174. A $1 bill is 156 mm long and can't be drawn to scale next to coins on a phone. That doesn't matter, since 2-year-olds already tell paper from coins.
- **Screens and real coins.** Transfer from a touch screen to the real thing is weak at 2 and 3 and better with a grown-up in the loop (sorting doc section 7).

**Design implication:** Use realistic photos of the heads side, drawn at true relative size and scaled 1.15. Later, tapping a coin flips it to tails and back, so tails is something to explore, not something to be tested on. End a round with a card the grown-up reads: "Find a penny with a grown-up!"

## 6. Which coins a child will actually see in 2026

- **Pennies still circulate.** The Mint struck the last circulating penny on 12 November 2025. Pennies stay legal tender, and the Federal Reserve will keep recirculating the roughly 114 billion that exist ([Treasury FAQ](https://home.treasury.gov/news/featured-stories/penny-production-cessation-faqs); [US Mint](https://www.usmint.gov/news/press-releases/united-states-mint-hosts-historic-ceremonial-strike-for-final-production-of-the-circulating-one-cent-coin)). Stores are short of them, and some states now let cash totals round to the nearest nickel ([NACS, updated 2026-05-29](https://www.convenience.org/advocate-for-the-industry/issues/swipe-fees-and-payments/the-penny-shortage); [Vermont](https://tax.vermont.gov/news/guidance-retailers-and-consumers-phase-out-penny-cash)). A 3-year-old in 2026 will still find pennies in jars and couch cushions, and fewer each year.
- **Heads is stable, mostly.** Lincoln on the penny, Jefferson on the nickel, Roosevelt on the dime and Washington on the quarter have been on circulating coins for decades. The exception is 2026. For one year the dime shows Liberty in a Liberty cap, and the five quarters show Pilgrims, Washington, Jefferson, Madison and Lincoln. The penny and nickel keep their designs with a dual date ([Semiquincentennial coinage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Semiquincentennial_coinage); I could not open the Mint's own page). A 2026 quarter can have Lincoln or Jefferson on it, the faces a child would link to the penny and nickel.
- **Tails varies a lot on the quarter.** State quarters, America the Beautiful, American Women quarters from 2022 to 2025, the 2026 set, and youth sports designs planned for 2027 to 2030.
- **Dollar coins are rare in change.** The Sacagawea dollar sees limited circulation ([coin table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_the_United_States_dollar)). A child meets "a dollar" as a green bill.

**Design implication:** Show the classic heads designs: Lincoln penny, Jefferson nickel, Roosevelt dime, Washington quarter. Keep the penny. Use the $1 bill, not the dollar coin, and leave out the half-dollar. Varied quarter tails and the 2026 designs could be a later "all kinds of quarters" pack. They don't belong at the start, because they break the one-face-one-coin rule a 3-year-old is still learning.

## 7. Safety

- Coins made up 62% of foreign-body swallowing cases in children under 6 seen in US emergency departments from 1995 to 2015, and 1-year-olds were the peak age ([Orsagh-Yentis et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2018-1988)). The AAP names coins, with food and toys, among the main causes of choking in young children ([AAP 2010](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2009-2862)). Every US coin fits through the small-parts test cylinder, which is 31.7 mm wide.
- The Illinois tip sheet says "coins can be a choking hazard so coin activities must be adult supervised" ([Illinois Early Learning](https://illinoisearlylearning.org/tipsheets/coins/)).

**Design implication:** The screen lets a 2 or 3-year-old sort coins alone. The real-world card at the end of a round says to do it with a grown-up.

## Recommendations for the spec

1. The Game teaches coin names, the idea that coins and bills are money, and sorting by look. It does not teach value.
2. The main task is Piggy Banks. Coins appear one at a time and the child drags each into the bank with that coin's photo on the front. A tap on a bank also places it. All the Tidy Up rules carry over: a soft bump and slide back on a wrong bank, the coin going in by itself on the second miss, a 7 s idle hint, one pointer, no buzzer.
3. Each landing plays a clink Sound and the coin's Word clip: "penny". The clip is the coin name alone, so the name is the last word heard. Tapping a bank plays its name.
4. Order of pairs: penny/quarter, then penny/dime, then dime/quarter, then 3 banks with penny/dime/quarter, then coins against the $1 bill, then add the nickel, first against the penny and last against the quarter. The grown-up picks the coins with one tap each on the first screen, as in Tidy Up's Practice, and nothing waits for a score.
5. The second task is Find the Coin. The named coin appears among 2 others at most, starting with 2 in total. After a correct tap the coin grows and its name plays again. After a wrong tap, the tapped coin's name plays and the right one glows. This could be a Find It Topic instead of a mode, since Find It already asks "find the match among three".
6. Use realistic public-domain photos of the heads side: Lincoln penny, Jefferson nickel, Roosevelt dime, Washington quarter, and a $1 bill. Draw coins at true relative size scaled 1.15, so the dime is 129 px and the quarter 174 px.
7. Tapping a resting coin flips it to tails and back. Tails never decides where a coin goes.
8. The reward is the banks. Each coin drops in with a clink, the bank gets fuller, and at the end the full bank wobbles and jingles. Use no stickers, points or stars.
9. A later Shop mode for 4-year-olds: drag one coin to the counter, get one thing. Any coin works and there are no prices.
10. End each round with a grown-up card: "Find a penny with a grown-up!"
11. Leave out values, cents, the ¢ and $ signs, "worth more", mixed-coin counting, change, the dollar coin, the half-dollar, and the 2026 and varied-tails designs.

## Open questions and weak evidence

- **One old study.** The ages and order come from Edmunds & Whitehurst: about 12 children per age, one US city, around 1973. The dip at 4 is their observation with no test of the cause. I found nothing newer on US coin naming under 5.
- **Penny first, for now.** The penny was the first coin named when children handled pennies every day. With production ended and cash at 14% of payments, the order may be shifting. The quarter may be more familiar to some children today. Watch which coin the user's daughter names first.
- **Nickel last** rests on confusions seen at 4 to 6, and no one has tested it at 3.
- **Berti & Bombi and Strauss** I read only through summaries.
- **Both sides and true size** have no study with young children. The recommendations follow from the size cue and from standards for older children.
- **Number of choices** comes from word-learning studies of new words at 24 and 30 months, not coins.
- **Word clips.** Commons has US-English pronunciations for penny, nickel, quarter and dollar ([En-us-penny.ogg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:En-us-penny.ogg) and siblings). I found no En-us-dime.ogg, so dime needs another source.
- **Paywalls.** I read only abstracts for Horst, Scott & Pollard, Simcock & DeLoache, Orsagh-Yentis and the AAP choking statement.
